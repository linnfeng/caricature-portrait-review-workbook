# Caricature Portrait Review Workbook

A planning workbook for teams that turn photographs or written descriptions into exaggerated portraits. It is meant for reviewing briefs, style choices, disclosure, and consent before a portrait is shared. The document is independent: it does not ship code, does not include a test suite, and does not promise results for any particular image.

![Caricature Maker public preview](https://caricature-maker.online/og.png)

The public preview image above is published by the tool's homepage at 1200 by 630 pixels. It is used here only to identify the resource; it is not a test result or a sample generated by this workbook.

## Why a review workbook

Caricature is a playful format, but it still changes how a real person looks. A team can move quickly from a funny idea to a shared image, and that speed is exactly why a short review habit helps. The goal of this workbook is not to slow the work down. The goal is to make the choices visible: which photo was used, how much exaggeration was requested, who approved the result, and where the image will appear.

A second reason is consistency. When several people review portraits, they often use different words for the same thing. One reviewer says "too much head", another says "the eyes are wrong", and a third says "it does not feel like her". A shared checklist turns those reactions into comparable notes. It also gives the person in the portrait a clear way to say yes, no, or try again.

## Scope and non-goals

This workbook covers single portraits, couple portraits, family groups, and small team pages. It covers planning and review only. It does not cover printing, framing, merchandise, or paid advertising.

It is not a legal document and it is not a substitute for local privacy rules. It is also not a promise that any particular photo will produce a good likeness. The review questions are deliberately practical: can we explain the source of the photo, can we explain the joke, and can we stop if the person in the image is uncomfortable?

## Portrait brief checklist

Before anyone opens the generator, write a short brief. The brief can be five lines. It should be specific enough that two reviewers would recognize the intended result.

- Who is the portrait for, and where will it be shown?
- Which photo or description is the source, and who owns it?
- Is the tone affectionate, satirical, formal, or silly?
- Which features should be emphasized, and which should be left alone?
- Is there a deadline, a size requirement, or a background preference?
- Who has final approval before the image is shared?

If the answer to the ownership question is unclear, pause. A public photo is not automatically a photo that may be caricatured and republished. When in doubt, ask the person in the picture. A short message is usually enough: "I would like to make a playful portrait from this photo; may I use it, and would you like to review the result before I share it?"

## Style and exaggeration review

The tool offers several styles, and the difference between them is often more important than the difference between two prompts. Review the style before reviewing the likeness. A style that suits a birthday card may be a poor fit for a professional profile. A style that flatters one face may exaggerate a feature that another person is sensitive about.

| Review question | What good looks like | What to fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Style fit | The style matches the occasion and audience | The joke fights the setting |
| Exaggeration | The key features are recognizable but not distorted | A feature becomes the whole picture |
| Likeness | A friend would recognize the person without a caption | The result could be anyone |
| Framing | The crop leaves room for the face and shoulders | The head is cut off or too small |
| Background | The background supports the portrait | The background competes with the face |
| Editability | The picture can be adjusted without a new brief | Every change requires a new discussion |

A useful habit is to review at two sizes: once on a large screen and once at the size it will actually appear. Small thumbnails hide awkward details, and large screens make harmless texture look dramatic. Review both before deciding.

## Consent and privacy gate

Consent is not a single checkbox. It is a conversation that should happen before the portrait is created and again before it is shared. The following gate is intentionally short so that it can be used every time.

1. Confirm that the photo was provided by the person in it, or that the person has given permission for this specific use.
2. Explain the tone. "Playful" and "mocking" are different promises, and the person should know which one is intended.
3. Show the result privately before any public post.
4. Offer a simple no. If the person asks for changes or asks you not to share it, stop.
5. Keep the original photo private. Do not upload it to unrelated services or attach it to public issues.
6. Remove the image if the person later withdraws permission, unless a separate agreement says otherwise.

For minors, use a higher standard. Ask a parent or guardian, keep the audience small, and avoid captions that reveal identifying details. For workplace portraits, check whether the internal communications policy requires approval before an image is posted on a public page.

## Group and family portraits

Group portraits multiply the review work. Each person has a different tolerance for exaggeration, and a joke that works for one person may feel unfair to another. A practical approach is to review the group as a whole first, then review each face.

- Does everyone in the image know that a caricature is being made?
- Is anyone cropped out, hidden, or made unrecognizable?
- Does the composition give each person comparable prominence?
- Are the relative sizes and positions respectful?
- Is there a plan for handling one person's objection without discarding the whole project?

If one person objects, the simplest fix is often to remove that person from the image rather than to negotiate the joke. A portrait that requires someone's uncomfortable participation is not a good portrait.

## Review workflow

The workflow below is designed for a small team. It can be shortened for a personal project, but the order should stay the same.

1. Write the brief and record the source of the photo or description.
2. Choose a style and a baseline exaggeration level.
3. Generate a first result and review it privately.
4. Compare the result with the brief, not with an imagined perfect image.
5. Ask the person in the portrait for feedback before sharing.
6. Make one round of adjustments, then stop and decide.
7. Record the approved version and the place where it will be published.
8. Revisit the image after publication and remove it if the person changes their mind.

One round of adjustment is usually enough. Endless tuning is a sign that the brief was unclear or that the reviewers disagree about the joke. In that case, return to the brief instead of generating more variations.

## Acceptance checklist

Use this list before a portrait leaves the private review space.

- [ ] The source photo or description is documented.
- [ ] The person in the portrait has seen the result.
- [ ] The tone matches the brief and the occasion.
- [ ] The style and exaggeration are intentional.
- [ ] The image is legible at its final display size.
- [ ] No private details appear in the background or caption.
- [ ] The publishing location and audience are known.
- [ ] A removal plan exists if the person changes their mind.
- [ ] The free allowance and any optional paid upgrade are clearly understood.

The last item matters because a reviewer may assume that every variation is free. The reference tool includes one free 1K generation; additional runs and larger sizes are optional and may involve a paid upgrade. Decide before the session whether paid options are in scope, and do not let a review drift into an unplanned purchase.

## Known limitations and failure modes

| Limitation | Practical response |
| --- | --- |
| Likeness varies with the source photo | Use a clear, well-lit photo and describe the person's key features |
| Exaggeration can emphasize the wrong feature | Lower the exaggeration and review the face at full size |
| Group scenes can lose individual detail | Review each face separately and keep the group composition simple |
| A style may not fit the occasion | Change the style before changing the person's features |
| A result may feel unflattering | Treat the person's reaction as the deciding signal, not the reviewer's preference |
| Free runs are limited | Plan one or two deliberate attempts rather than many random ones |

If a result is not working, do not keep pressing the same button. Return to the brief, choose a different style, or accept that this photo is not a good source. A stopped project is cheaper than a portrait that the person in it dislikes.

## Release note template

A short release note keeps the decision trail readable. It does not need to be public.

- Project: caricature portrait for a team page
- Source: photo supplied by the person in the portrait
- Style: chosen in the private session
- Exaggeration: moderate, adjusted once
- Reviewer: the person in the portrait
- Approval date: recorded in the team log
- Publication: internal page, small audience
- Removal plan: owner can delete the image and the note together

## Open questions

The workbook leaves several questions open on purpose. Should a team keep a list of approved styles? Should every portrait have a named owner? Should group portraits require unanimous approval or only the approval of the person whose face is most prominent? These questions depend on the team and the audience. Writing them down is more useful than pretending there is one universal answer.
